Hope Turtle Project

Turtle Wrangler

An Android app that puts the turtle's cockpit in your pocket — see what it sees, and steer it from your phone, without ever touching the tiny screen on the hull.

Proposal — not yet built

The problem today

Right now, everything about checking on or adjusting a Hope Turtle happens through a single small screen and a single button on the hull.

Today

  • A 1.3-inch OLED screen, one action button for control
  • Different numbers of clicks open different menus — single, double, triple, quadruple, or a long hold — genuinely tedious in practice
  • The OLED washes out and can't be read in direct daylight
  • Checking battery, sail, GPS and heading means clicking through several separate screens, one at a time
  • Setting a destination or starting a journey is a multi-step button sequence you have to remember
  • Any control at all is impossible unless you're physically right beside the turtle, in the water
  • If a stamp or a command fails, the tiny screen is easy to miss — or it gives no reason at all

With Turtle Wrangler

  • A full phone screen, way easier to read outdoors than the OLED!
  • Tap what you want — no click-counting to memorize
  • Works from up to 5 metres away — no wading in beside the hull
  • Battery, sail, heading, and position all visible together, at a glance
  • Setting a destination or starting a journey is a couple of taps from a distance
  • Manual GPS stamps are one big button — and the turtle tells you straight away if one didn't take
  • Every command gets an answer: done, or exactly why not

What the app actually does

Four simple tabs plus a settings page, each replacing a part of what the on-hull screen does today — just easier to read and easier to use. Every button in the app does something the turtle's own button can already do, so nothing is lost when a phone isn't around.

📊

Dashboard

One glance tells you heading, battery level, sail angle, GPS position, and how far the turtle still has to travel — live, the moment your phone is near it.

🧭

Navigate

Set where the turtle should head, start or end a test run, and see how far off-course it currently is — all with taps instead of button-click codes.

📍

GPS stamps

Switch the turtle to manual logging and record its position with one big tap — each stamp is confirmed, counted, and tracked until it reaches the website.

🔧

Diagnostics

Run a quick sail test to find the wind, and check at a glance that every sensor on board — compass, battery monitor, GPS — is healthy before launch.

⚙️

Settings

Hand the turtle its WiFi details and adjust how often it reports home — no laptop or cable required in the field.

🔒

Safe for real voyages

You sign in with your Buwana account first, and the app only opens turtles you look after. Pairing still needs a 6-digit code shown on the turtle itself, and before a real mission Bluetooth can be switched off completely.

How it would work, in practice

1

Sign in once

Log in with your Buwana account, the same one you use on hopeturtles.org. The app loads the turtles you look after and remembers them, so it keeps working out at the shore with no signal.

2

Walk up with your phone

Open the app anywhere within about 5 metres of the turtle. No WiFi network or internet connection is needed at this point.

3

It finds your turtle

The app finds the turtle over Bluetooth (the same short-range wireless a pair of headphones uses) and checks that it's one of yours. The first time, you pair by typing the 6-digit code the turtle shows on its own screen.

4

See it, tap it, done

Live readings appear immediately. Any change you make — a new destination, a GPS stamp, a sail test — reaches the turtle in under a second, and the turtle answers back: done, or exactly why not.

Why this is worth doing

Faster field checks. A full status readout in seconds instead of cycling through several tiny screens.

Wayyyy less tedious. No more misremembering whether it's a double-click or triple-click to reach a setting.

Easier to train new team members. A phone app is familiar; a button-code sequence on a 1-inch screen is not.

Better diagnostics before launch. One screen confirms every sensor is healthy before a turtle goes in the water.

No extra hardware. Any Android phone works — nothing new to buy or carry.

No silent failures. Every tap gets a clear answer from the turtle — "no GPS fix yet", "clock not set" — instead of a screen that just doesn't change.

Locked down. You sign in before you see any turtle, Bluetooth only opens for a few minutes at a time, pairing needs the code on the hull, and it can be switched off entirely for a real mission.

Doesn't replace the shore tracking system. This is for hands-on fieldwork; the existing website tracking stays exactly as is.

What building this involves

Three pieces of work, all needed for the first version: one on each side of the Bluetooth connection, and a small one on the website.

Turtle side · built

A firmware update

Done: the turtle now broadcasts its data over Bluetooth and accepts commands, as an addition to the existing system rather than a rebuild of it. What's left is testing it on real turtles from the app.

Phone side

A new Android app

Built from scratch, but a well-understood kind of app — the screens sketched above, talking to the turtle over Bluetooth.

Website side · needed first

Sign-in and turtle lists

A small, read-only addition to hopeturtles.org. The app signs in with Buwana, gets the list of turtles you look after, and shows their latest shore reports when they're out of Bluetooth range.

Technical · Turtle side

The turtleOS firmware update

turtleOS (v2.4.1, MicroPython 1.28 on the XIAO ESP32-S3) gains a Bluetooth Low Energy peripheral role: the turtle advertises, a phone connects, live readings go out as notifications, and commands come in as writes that each return a result. It sits alongside the existing WiFi telemetry to hopeturtles.org. That pipeline doesn't change.

✓ Phases 0–7 built ✓ Pairing tested on turtle 18 On-device command tests: waiting for the app

What's new in the firmware

src/net/ble_service.py

Owns the radio: switching it on and off, advertising under the turtle's name, tracking the connection, pairing, and the "wrangle window". The Bluetooth interrupt handler only records events. All real work happens on the main loop's background tick.

src/net/ble_telemetry.py

Packs eight live characteristics (position, nav, targets, sail, power, IMU, status, shore sync) from data the firmware already holds. Values are sent only when they change and only while a phone is connected.

src/net/ble_commands.py

Runs the command channel: 21 opcodes, one command at a time, and exactly one final result per command. 33,000 fuzzed writes produced zero crashes.

src/app/actions.py

One shared action layer. Each OLED button gesture and its Bluetooth command call the same function, so the app and the hull can never drift apart in behaviour.

The contract between turtle and phone

Both sides build against GATT contract v1, documented in turtleOS at docs/wrangler/README.md. It has three services:

ServiceUUIDWhat it carries
Turtle26d00001-…Contract version, turtle name, and eight read + notify characteristics for live state. Readable by any nearby phone.
Command26d00002-…A Command write (opcode, seq, payload) and a Result notify (opcode, seq, code, payload). This covers journeys, destinations, GPS stamps, sail sweeps, WiFi credentials, sleep and reboot.
Device Information0x180AThe standard Bluetooth service: manufacturer, model, firmware version and serial (device_id).
  • Compact binary on the wire. Values are little-endian struct packs, scaled integers stand in for floats (heading × 10, lat/lon × 10⁷), and every value fits in 20 bytes.
  • Missing data is explicit. No GPS fix sends a sentinel (e.g. 0x7FFFFFFF), never a fake zero.
  • Forward-compatible. Firmware can add fields or opcodes without breaking older apps. Only a breaking change bumps the contract version.

Security and power

  • Commands need a paired phone. Pairing uses LE Secure Connections: the turtle shows a 6-digit code on its OLED and you type it into the phone. You have to be standing at the hull to see the code. Live readings stay open to any phone.
  • Pairing only works while the turtle's Bluetooth screen is open. Bonds are saved in /ble_bonds.json and survive a reboot.
  • Wrangle window. The turtle advertises for 10 minutes after boot or wake, or when the Bluetooth screen is opened, then goes quiet. Before a mission voyage, Bluetooth can be switched off entirely, and only the turtle's button can turn it back on.
Still to do on the turtle side: run the deferred on-device test checklist (best done from the Wrangler app itself), measure current draw on battery to settle the 10-minute window default, and add a cross-track-error calculation (the Nav characteristic sends "no data" for it today).

Wrangler gets its own repository, h2hproject/wrangler, with an independent codebase and release cycle. It still depends on two other projects: turtleOS defines how the app talks to a turtle, and hopeturtles.org (through Buwana) decides who you are and which turtles are yours.

Languages and stack

The app is written entirely in Kotlin, with the UI in Jetpack Compose and the build in Gradle's Kotlin DSL. There is no Java and no XML layouts. We're going native instead of using Flutter or React Native because Bluetooth is the core of this app, and Android's BLE quirks are where the bugs live: one GATT operation at a time, bonding, MTU and reconnects. A cross-platform plugin would add a layer exactly there. An iPhone version (v2.0) would be a Swift / Core Bluetooth app either way.

Yes, a local database — as a field notebook

The phone is often offline at the shoreline, so Wrangler keeps a small Room database. It is a cache and a log, not a second source of truth. The turtle still delivers its own telemetry to hopeturtles.org, and the app never uploads readings on the turtle's behalf.

Login first: Buwana before any turtle data

The app opens on a Buwana sign-in screen. Nothing about any turtle shows until you're signed in. After that, the app only connects to, and shows, turtles you manage (turtles_tb.turtle_manager), or every turtle if you're an admin. That's the same rule the website's my-turtle page uses.

  • Matching a turtle to your account. A turtle's Bluetooth serial number (Device Information 0x180A) is its turtle_id, so the app checks it against your downloaded turtle list right after connecting. Turtles that aren't yours are listed as "not on your account" and don't open.
  • Works without signal after the first sign-in. Shorelines often have no coverage, so the first sign-in needs internet and later sessions don't. The app keeps a long-lived, revocable session token in the Keystore and caches your turtle list in Room, refreshing both whenever it's online.
  • What login does and doesn't protect. Login gates the app. The turtle's radio is a separate matter: live Bluetooth readings are open to any nearby scanner by design, and commands are protected by passkey pairing at the hull. If readings should be private too, turtleOS can require pairing for reads as well. That is a small firmware change (marking the characteristics encrypted-read) plus a contract update, at the cost of every phone having to pair before it sees anything. Also, the website already publishes turtle positions openly through its public endpoints, so app login alone doesn't make that data private.
  • Accountability. Knowing who is signed in means commands sent from the app (journey started, destination changed, WiFi pushed) can later be logged against a person.

New APIs, not fixes to old ones

The existing APIs are fine for what they do, and none of them need changing. They just serve different callers:

  • Device API (/api/v1/*) authenticates as a turtle using that turtle's device key. It stays turtle-only: putting device keys in an APK would let anyone post telemetry as that turtle.
  • Website endpoints for your own turtles rely on a 15-minute browser session cookie, which doesn't fit a phone app that has to work offline for days.

So the work is a new, small, read-only namespace in this repository, /api/app/v1, behind a new bearer-token middleware (middleware/appAuth.js). It reuses the existing models and access rules rather than duplicating them.

EndpointDoes
POST /api/app/v1/sessionThe app sends the ID token it got from Buwana. The server verifies it against Buwana's JWKS (as the website login does), upserts the user, and returns a revocable app token. Only a hash of the token is stored, using the same pattern as turtle device keys.
DELETE /api/app/v1/sessionSign out and revoke the token.
GET /api/app/v1/meThe signed-in user: name, role and earthling emoji.
GET /api/app/v1/turtlesTurtles you manage (all for admins): turtle_id, name, mission and status, which the app uses to match nearby turtles.
GET /api/app/v1/turtles/:id/latestThe newest shore report: position, battery, heading and last-seen time.
GET /api/app/v1/turtles/:id/telemetry?since=Track history. Not needed for v1; it powers the voyage-track map later.

Supporting changes:

  • Small tweaks to existing code: one migration for an app-sessions table, and letting validateIdToken accept Wrangler's client ID as an audience alongside the website's.
  • On Buwana: register Wrangler as its own public client (PKCE, no secret), with redirect org.hopeturtles.wrangler:/oauth2redirect.
  • Open questions for Buwana: does authorize.php accept a custom-scheme redirect? If not, an https App Link on hopeturtles.org can hand off to the app. Does Buwana issue refresh tokens? If it doesn't, the server-issued app token is what keeps users signed in offline.

Keeping three repositories in step

  • Contract first. Any change to the Bluetooth format lands in turtleOS's contract doc before either side's code changes. The app reads the contract version on every connect and goes read-only if the turtle is newer than it understands.
  • Shared test vectors. Wrangler's decoder tests use the same captured byte strings that turtleOS's tests/ble_decode.py understands, so a packing change on one side fails a test on the other.
  • No hardware needed to start. Early app work can test against an nRF Connect server profile or a small bleak script on a laptop, though the real firmware is already available.
